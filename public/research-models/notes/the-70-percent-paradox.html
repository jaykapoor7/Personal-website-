Note 01 / Spatial epidemiology

The 70% Paradox

Two cities vaccinate 70% of their dogs. Are they equally protected?

Open experiment 01
FIG 01
QuestionDataModelLimitsIn practiceSources

The question

What changes when two places vaccinate the same fraction of dogs but leave different patterns of susceptibility? WHO discusses a roughly 70% coverage target for dog vaccination campaigns. Ferguson and colleagues analyzed vaccination and contact-tracing data from the Serengeti district and found that spatial gaps in coverage were associated with poorer rabies control. The experiment isolates the geometry of that idea.

From the StrayPaw register

The Mission Rabies campaign contributes 6,462 historical vaccination records. We grouped them by archived H3 cell and used cells containing at least 20 vaccination records. The top 15 of 72 eligible cells contained 2,633 of 6,172 eligible records. To set the starting slider, divide the observed share by the top cells’ share of eligible cells, subtract 1, multiply by 0.75, and clamp to 0–1. That gives 79% after rounding. This mapping is a display choice. Record concentration reflects where vaccinations were documented and lacks the local dog population denominators needed to measure coverage. No individual locations are used in the interactive figure.

How the model works

A 20 × 20 grid has up to eight local neighbors per dog, including diagonals, with no wrapping at the edges. Vaccination gives immediate complete protection in the model. Each run starts with one dog drawn from the susceptible dogs in that layout. The two cities share the same random ordering for choosing that dog and the same edge-specific transmission draws. The 0.42 probability is an illustrative teaching value.

Coverage

280 / 400 = 70% in both cities

Layout score

scoreᵢ = (1 − c)uᵢ + c xᵢ

Vaccinate the 280 lowest scores. uᵢ is a fixed pseudorandom rank, xᵢ is a normalized horizontal position with a small row offset, and c is the slider value.
One infectious generation

P(infection | k infectious neighbors) = 1 − (1 − 0.42)ᵏ

Here k counts infectious neighbors making contact with the susceptible dog in that generation. An infectious dog then leaves the transmitting population.

How far the result goes

The model stops when transmission ends. An outbreak size includes the initial infection. The displayed range is the empirical 10th–90th percentile of 64 runs on the same layouts, not a confidence interval. These grids represent separate cities without movement between them. The model omits incubation, roaming, waning immunity and repeated incursions.

What to measure in practice

A municipal report can pair the citywide coverage estimate with ward-level denominators and mapped vaccination gaps. The quality of both the dog population estimate and the location records matters.
